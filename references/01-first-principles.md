## Reason up from fundamental truths, not by analogy

> "It is physics. You know, the sort of first principles reasoning. Generally I think there are -- what I mean
> by that is, boil things down to their fundamental truths and reason up from there, as opposed to reasoning
> by analogy. Through most of our life, we get through life by reasoning by analogy, which essentially means
> copying what other people do with slight variations." [TED13]

> "Reasoning from first principles is a superpower" [X 2025-02-17](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/1891351204862783701)

**The method, in practice:**
1. Write down what you're trying to achieve.
2. Strip away "how it's usually done". List only what physics, chemistry, maths or the customer actually
   require.
3. Rebuild from those truths. Compare the result with the conventional answer.
4. Where they differ, the gap is the opportunity.

(The four steps are our summary of the quote above. The examples in chapters 02 to 04 show him applying it.)

## Think in the limit

> "For something important you need to reason with from first principles and think about things in the limit
> one direction or the other." [LEX400 01:10:59]

His example: "even if you harness the entire power of the sun, you'll still care about useful compute per
watt." [LEX400 01:10:59] **The move:** push a variable to its extreme (zero cost, infinite scale, the whole
market) and see what still matters.

> "You're not going to be right every time, but you want to minimize how often you're confidently wrong."
> [LEX400 00:37:02]

## The question is harder than the answer

> "Sometimes the answer is arguably the easy part, trying to frame the question correctly is the hard part.
> Once you frame the question correctly, the answer is often easy." [LEX438 01:21:02]

> "And Adams makes the point that it's actually the question that is harder than the answer." [TED22]
> (On Douglas Adams.)

## Ask for negative feedback

> "Then also to really pay attention to negative feedback, and solicit it, particularly from friends. This may
> sound like simple advice, but hardly anyone does that, and it's incredibly helpful." [TED13]

**Checks to run:**
1. What's the conventional answer to your problem? Which parts of it are physics, and which are habit?
2. Push one variable to its limit. What still matters?
3. What would you need to see to be proven wrong? Have you asked a friend to look for it?
