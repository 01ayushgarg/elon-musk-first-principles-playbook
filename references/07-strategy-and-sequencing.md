# 07 · Strategy: sequence the plan, then focus

## The Secret Master Plan (2006)

> "The strategy of Tesla is to enter at the high end of the market, where customers are prepared to pay a
> premium, and then drive down market as fast as possible to higher unit volume and lower prices with each
> successive model." [MP1]

His reason: almost any new technology starts with a high unit cost before it can be optimised, and electric
cars were no exception [MP1]. Free cash flow went back into R&D to bring the cheaper models sooner [MP1].

His four-line summary, as written in the post [MP1]:

> Build sports car
> Use that money to build an affordable car
> Use that money to build an even more affordable car
> While doing above, also provide zero emission electric power generation options
> Don't tell anyone.

## Part Deux (2016): explain the bigger picture

He wrote the second plan "to explain how our actions fit into a larger picture, so that they would seem less
random." [MP2] Of the start he wrote: "I thought our chances of success were so low that I didn't want to risk
anyone's funds in the beginning but my own." [MP2] His SpaceX odds in 2002 were similar: maybe a 10% chance of
even reaching orbit [NS17].

## Make your own products redundant

At IAC 2017, his answer to how SpaceX would pay for its next rocket:

> "if we can build a system that cannibalizes our own products, makes our own products redundant, then all of
> the resources, which are quite enormous, that are used for Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy and Dragon, can be applied
> to one system." [IAC17]

In the 2016 version of the plan, he expected launch revenue (satellites and space-station cargo) to produce
"a pretty decent net cash flow" toward Mars, and said he was accumulating assets personally to fund it [NS17].

**What the sources don't say.** It's often said that Starlink exists to fund Starship. In these sources he
doesn't say that in so many words. In the 2026 interview, the host frames Starlink as the revenue step for
Falcon 9 and orbital data centres as the possible one for Starship, and he doesn't take up the framing
[DW 00:00:00]. We report it as the host's framing, not his.

## Open the patents when the mission is bigger than the company

In 2014 he took down the wall of patents in Tesla's lobby. His reasoning: Tesla existed to accelerate
sustainable transport, and laying "intellectual property landmines" behind it would work against that goal. He
added that Tesla alone could never build cars fast enough for a fleet of about 2 billion [PAT14].

> "Technology leadership is not defined by patents, which history has repeatedly shown to be small protection
> indeed against a determined competitor, but rather by the ability of a company to attract and motivate the
> world's most talented engineers." [PAT14]

**Our reading:** the moat he names is people (chapter 08), not paperwork.

## The tech tree

> "So, there's kind of a tech tree, if you will. You've got the basics. You need literacy before you can have
> Lord of the Rings." [LEX438 00:16:18]

For Neuralink he said the sensible start was "basic neuron damage issues" before anything grander
[LEX438 00:17:58].

## Focus ruthlessly on the next milestone

> "We need to be super focused on getting to orbit then super focused on getting the ship back, then we can
> worry about doors." [SB2 28:06 to 28:12]

Nest the goals, each measured in time: the top goal was "fastest time to a city on Mars", with "fastest time to
orbit" as a subset [SB3 16:42].

## Constrain the problem

He scoped Hyperloop to "high traffic city pairs that are less than about 1500 km or 900 miles apart", leaving
longer trips to supersonic planes [HYP13]. **Our reading:** naming where your idea is the wrong answer is part of
the strategy.

---

## How to apply it (our suggestion)

1. **Write the end state in one line** (his: a self-sustaining city on Mars; sustainable transport).
2. **Write four steps,** each funded by the one before. Step 1 is where customers pay a premium today.
3. **For each step, name the unit cost that must fall** before the next is possible, and by how much.
4. **Ask what you'd cannibalise.** Which of your products should the next one make redundant?
5. **Write the one paragraph** that makes your moves look less random to an outsider. Publish it if you can.
6. **Name the doors**: features that can wait until the next milestone.

### Worked scenario (fictional)

A company making lab-grade air sensors for $2,400.

| Line | Funded by | Unit cost must fall |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Lab sensor for research groups ($2,400) | Grants and lab budgets | (start) |
| 2. Building sensor for offices ($400) | Step 1 margins, and its calibration data | From $900 to $150 |
| 3. Home sensor ($79) | Step 2 volume | From $150 to $30 |
| 4. While doing above, publish an open air-quality map | All three | Data cost per reading |

Cannibalise: the building sensor is designed so that labs will eventually prefer ten of them to one lab unit.
Doors: no app for step 1; researchers export CSV. Patents: the sensor format is opened in step 3, if the map
matters more than the margin.

### Failure modes

- **Starting at step 3.** The cheap version first, before costs have fallen.
- **Four steps not funded by each other.** Then it's a wish list, not a plan.
- **Protecting a product the next one should replace.**
- **Building the door.** Polishing features before the milestone that matters.

### Limits

His plans ran over many years, and a four-line plan is a direction, not a forecast. Tesla's 2023 and
2025 master plans are signed by "The Tesla Team", not by Musk, so they are not quoted here. Opening patents made
sense for a mission he wanted others to join; most companies' patents protect a margin they need.
